Why exactly 48?

Little's law gives the ceiling

ceiling  = pool size ÷ hold time
inside   = 10 ÷ 0.2065 s = 48.4 RPS
measured = 47.8 RPS

A connection is checked out the moment the transaction begins and returned only on commit. Put a 200 ms HTTP call in the middle and those 200 ms are a connection's 200 ms.

A bigger pool doesn't fix it

600 RPS would need 600 × 0.2065 = 124 connections; Postgres max_connections is 100. And if the provider slowed to 2 s, 100 RPS would need 200 connections — your capacity would be set by a company you don't control.

Outside, the bottleneck moves

With no connection held during the wait, the limit becomes real database work: at 1000 RPS hold time grows from 2 ms to 17.6 ms (WAL, commit, indexes) → 10 ÷ 0.0176 ≈ 570 RPS. Measured safe ceiling: 596.

A pool near its ceiling is fragile

On a cold JVM even 20 RPS produced 9% errors: a queued request still holds its connection for the full 200 ms once it gets one, so the queue never drains itself. The system can stay collapsed under traffic it could normally serve — metastable failure.

Environment: Docker Desktop (8 CPU / 8 GB), postgres:17, WireMock provider fixed at 200 ms, payment-service with a 10-connection pool, Hikari connection timeout 3 s, virtual threads on.
